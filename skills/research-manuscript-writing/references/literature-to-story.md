# Literature investigation and story development

Use this preparation mode for a first draft starting from an idea, or when the central framing needs substantive investigation. Follow the author's permitted sources, time horizon, and output scope. It is a focused narrative investigation, not automatically a systematic review. Reuse traceable prior work instead of restarting a completed search. Grammar edits and bounded source-based revisions need only their relevant checks.

## Turn the idea into a question that can change

Recover the actual phenomenon, task or interpretive question, setting, proposed scientific move, and available study evidence. Separate the author's conjecture from an established result. Identify what would make the idea useful and what existing finding could undermine its claimed need. Keep the target venue and audience in view, but do not shape the field record to suit a publication pitch. If the study has no results yet, develop a rationale or provisional outline; do not write completed findings.

Check that the question can be answered by the proposed method and available material. A better narrative cannot supply an absent comparison, proof or source analysis. Keep an unresolved research decision distinct from an editorial repair, and ask the author only when a consequential choice cannot be inferred from the brief. Do not replace that choice with a flattering feasibility score.

Break the question into searchable concepts and alternative disciplinary terms. Search the problem and prior solutions as well as the proposed method. Explore neighboring formulations, contrary findings, and work that already solves part or all of the difficulty. Broaden or narrow queries according to the relevance of results, not a universal hit-count threshold. An absent exact phrase or an untried combination of named components does not establish a meaningful gap.

## Choose discovery routes for the field

Choose sources for their coverage of the question and the evidence needed. Biomedical questions may call for specialist indexes and original studies; computing or engineering may need conference proceedings, journals and preprints; theoretical work may need proofs and foundational articles; humanities or social research may require books, disciplinary indexes, local-language work or primary archives. These are examples, not source rankings or mandatory database lists. Search broadly enough to find work outside the author's preferred venue or research network.

Use available search tools, scholarly indexes and citation links to locate candidates, then inspect authoritative records and the papers themselves. Give recent relevant work deliberate attention, and follow its backward references and newer citing work where useful. Retain older foundational, stronger, or contradictory evidence. Record the actual search date, sources, queries, time coverage and material access limits. Distinguish a proposed search from an executed one. If a route fails, use an available lawful alternative and record the remaining coverage gap; do not silently exclude inaccessible pivotal work or bypass access restrictions.

Deduplicate candidate records by verified work and version identity, using identifiers and complete metadata rather than title resemblance alone. Preserve preprint-to-publication relationships and separately intended versions. Discovery is not a completed bibliographic authenticity audit. Keep identity, claim support, methodological quality and novelty as separate judgments. When a reference-authenticity check is required, use the available reference-authenticity-audit workflow, including its two independent Scholar-first checks, or explicitly report that verification incomplete. Do not substitute ordinary discovery for that contract or force a full authenticity audit of every discarded candidate.

## Read framing, then inspect what the study establishes

Read introductions and related work to learn the questions authors saw, what prior research enabled, how they connected findings, and why they considered a difficulty consequential. Use these as leads for investigation, not reusable prose or proof that their asserted gap is still open. Trace pivotal claims about earlier work to the original source rather than inheriting a later paper's characterization.

For sources that determine the story, read enough of the method, results, argument and limitations to judge the claim. Record the study setting, operative method or reasoning, assumptions, comparison or source selection, result and supported scope. Match appraisal to the research design. Experimental support, a theorem under assumptions, and an interpretation of archival material require different checks. Assess relevance, inferential support and methodological adequacy separately. Recency, prestige, peer-review status, citation counts and author affiliation cannot replace this reading or decide whether a conflicting result counts. Avoid fixed composite quality scores that conceal missing evidence.

Maintain a compact source record with a stable work/version ID and observed link; publication and verification status; what was actually available and read; question, method and supported finding; relevant source location; and implications for the current question. Label full text, selected sections, abstract-only and metadata-only access accurately. Keep author-stated limitations, your evidence-based inference, and unreported properties distinct. An abstract's silence cannot establish that a method lacks a capability. A source record need not become a long template for every background citation.

## Use independent overlap, then reciprocal challenge

For this preparation mode, use two or more separate investigation agents and a coordinating synthesis role. Give investigators the same neutral author brief and central question, plus complementary search emphases appropriate to the field. Both must independently examine the decisive gap and closest prior solutions; merely splitting unrelated topics does not provide cross-checking. With bounded supplied materials, both can independently examine those sources, but that is not independent retrieval or a live field survey.

Keep initial records separate until each investigator finishes. Do not provide a preferred gap verdict or the other agent's conclusions in the initial brief. Ask each for source-backed progress, the closest relevant solutions, challenges to the idea, candidate unresolved questions, and access limits. Assign leaf tasks explicitly so investigators do not recursively create duplicate teams. Shared papers and shared underlying datasets remain shared evidence even when several agents read them.

Then exchange the initial records. Each investigator checks the other's consequential claims against the underlying material, challenges overclaims and missing alternatives, and records what is supported, contradicted or unresolved. Reopen the relevant source or run a targeted additional search when a material dispute can be resolved within scope. Preserve the initial records and explain changed judgments. Agreement and majority votes are not evidence, and disagreement must not disappear into an averaged narrative. Stop when central disputes are resolved or explicitly bounded; do not impose endless debate rounds or demand consensus.

Before calling two findings contradictory, compare their question, population or setting, construct, measurement and conditions. A conditional difference or insufficient overlap may explain an apparent conflict without discrediting either study. Separate the kind of disagreement from whether it has been resolved. Criticism needs source support too; finding no material defect is valid, and there is no quota of objections or research gaps.

The coordinator builds one evidence map from the reconciled findings. The investigation is sufficient for a bounded draft when the closest relevant prior work, recent developments, necessary foundations and meaningful counterevidence have been considered, pivotal premises are traceable, and remaining uncertainty does not invalidate the proposed claim. This is a judgment about the stated coverage, not proof of exhaustive novelty. If access or evidence prevents that judgment, state the precise dependency and continue only the supported parts.

## Convert the field record into one manuscript argument

Separate established progress, documented limitations, plausible open questions, and what remains unknown because the search or source is incomplete. Ask whether a candidate gap matters scientifically and whether it survives the strongest relevant counterexample. Missing evidence is not itself proof of a missing capability. Do not infer why nobody studied something. If existing work defeats the proposed novelty, narrow the contribution, recognize replication or synthesis value, or leave a research decision to the author; do not invent a new distinguishing feature.

Build a compact story account connecting the relevant progress, unresolved question and its consequence, motivation for the scientific move, actual contribution, and evidence-supported answer. Each connection must be grounded. Let this account evolve from the investigation before selecting the final introduction and its connected literature threads. Avoid the circular workflow of fixing a gap first and keeping only papers that support it. Differences among prior studies can explain the field without framing each as a defect the present paper repairs.

Hand the writer a concise account of the question and study status, the source map with reading and verification limits, the selected argument and evidence behind its links, and any unresolved author decisions. Preserve the distinction between planned work, source-derived knowledge and results produced by this study. The writer should reuse that record and refresh only stale or affected claims. If a core premise remains unsupported, continue an explicitly provisional passage or outline and identify the exact missing evidence; do not turn a fatal gap in support into a routine limitations sentence and call the story ready.

Keep search logs, agent disagreements, alternative story candidates and unresolved research tasks in preparation notes. The manuscript carries the necessary scientific reasoning, decisive evidence and consequential scope. Do not substitute project-readiness or novelty-checking questions for its scientific question; keep unresolved choices about whether to pursue a design in the handoff. It does not narrate search diligence, argue with imagined reviewers, promise that all alternatives were considered, or attach a disclaimer to every claim. Do not hide a limitation that changes interpretation merely to sound confident. Write concise connected prose, retaining the shortest necessary bridge and removing repetitions that serve no new reader function.
