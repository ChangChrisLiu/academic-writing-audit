\begin{abstract}
Early bearing-fault detection supports timely inspection, but power constraints limit how often monitoring sensors can take measurements. Sparse, irregular readings may miss short fault transitions, and their interpretation depends on the time separating observations. We study whether incorporating this timing information can reduce detection delay without increasing the mean measurement count. Our supervised detector represents elapsed time together with sensor observations and trains on a labeled archived dataset without new exploratory measurements or a reward specification. In a held-out replay evaluation of 120 recorded fault episodes from three machines, the time-aware detector achieved a median detection delay of 3.2\,s, compared with 5.0\,s for the time-agnostic supervised baseline at the same mean measurement count. These retrospective results support the use of elapsed-time information for this detection task; prospective performance remains to be evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bearing monitoring must detect emerging faults early enough to arrange inspection while operating within the available sensing power. Taking fewer measurements conserves power, but increases the chance that a short fault transition occurs between readings. Irregular sampling adds a further challenge: similar sensor readings can be separated by substantially different intervals, changing the temporal context in which they should be interpreted. The question addressed here is how to use these sparse, irregular observations to reduce fault-detection delay without increasing the measurement budget.

Prior studies illustrate different approaches to this problem. A fixed-sampling study reports that short transitions between measurements can be missed~\cite{fixed26}. An adaptive approach learns when to measure through online trial-and-error using a fault-delay reward~\cite{adaptive26}. That training procedure requires live exploratory measurements and the specified reward. Supervised training on labeled archived observations offers another route: the detector in~\cite{archive25} requires neither exploratory interaction nor a reward specification. However, that classifier discards the elapsed time between readings. Its input representation therefore cannot distinguish otherwise identical observation sequences recorded at different intervals, even when those intervals are relevant to interpreting fault development.

We investigate a time-aware supervised detector that represents elapsed time together with sensor observations. This representation makes the spacing between readings available to the detector, allowing it to distinguish similar observations with different temporal contexts. Training uses a labeled archived dataset, without collecting new exploratory measurements. The study focuses on whether preserving elapsed-time information improves detection delay under the existing measurement budget.

We evaluate the detector through held-out replay of 120 recorded fault episodes from three machines. At the same mean measurement count, median detection delay was 3.2\,s for the time-aware detector and 5.0\,s for the time-agnostic supervised baseline. A secondary ablation omitting the elapsed-time encoder yielded a median delay of 4.8\,s, and runtime was below 2\,ms per observation on the test workstation. The primary finding is the lower median detection delay in this retrospective comparison. The evaluation does not establish prospective deployment performance or isolate a causal contribution from individual components.
